## When to Deploy the Hoist vs. Landing Skids Down

Every aerial rescue begins with a critical decision: should you touch down or deploy the cable? Landing directly on firm ground is always faster, safer, and less fuel-intensive than holding an out-of-ground-effect hover. However, dynamic mission environments regularly deny you that luxury.

Choosing to deploy the winch when you could simply land 40 yards away burns unnecessary fuel and exposes your crew to mechanical failure. Conversely, trying to force skids onto unstable terrain risks dynamic rollover. The matrix below outlines how seasoned flight crews determine their approach.

| Environmental Factor | Touchdown Landing Preferred | Hoist Extraction Mandatory |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Ground Incline** | Flat clearings, hard-packed dirt, paved pads (< 5° slope) | Steep hillsides, jagged cliff ledges, ravines (> 10° slope) |
| **Obstacle Density** | Open fields, wide highways, clear sports parks | Dense pine canopy, high-voltage power lines, antennas |
| **Surface Integrity** | Asphalt, dry turf, concrete, firm soil | Marshland, deep powder snow, moving water, soft mud |
| **Thermal / Fire Threat** | Distant brush fires with clear upwind visibility | Active structural burns, shifting embers, heavy smoke plumes |
| **Crowd Presence** | Controlled perimeters secured by ground police units | Uncontrolled civilian crowds or panic zones near the skids |

When evaluating the rescue scene, use your downwind leg to inspect approach and departure paths. If the site exhibits any red flags listed in the hoist column, establish an orbit and prepare the cabin for cable deployment.

## Crew Roles and Cabin Coordination

A successful hoist sortie is never a solo achievement. While single-pilot operations are possible during casual play, mastering high-difficulty missions requires tight coordination across a three-person team. In the standard crew setup, every seat bears distinct operational burdens that determine mission success.

```
       [ PILOT ]
   Holds Stable Hover
   Manages Fuel Bingo
          │
          ▼
 [ HOIST OPERATOR ] ◄── (Comms Link) ──► [ RESCUE MEDIC ]
 Controls Cable Pay/Reel                  Executes Ground Care
 Clears Drift Limits                      Secures Patient Litter
```

### Pilot (Flight Command)
The pilot’s sole job during a winch operation is station-keeping. You must neutralize translational drift, counteract crosswinds, and manage engine power margins. 

* **Flight Inputs:** Use micro-taps on your collective keys (`Shift` to gain lift, `Ctrl` to settle) rather than large, sweeping corrections.
* **Standard Callouts:** Announce `"On station, stable"` only when horizontal drift ceases; immediately call `"Drifting"` if the airframe slips.
* **Fuel Monitoring:** Hovers demand peak turbine power. Pilots must constantly track the fuel gauge and call out fuel states every 60 seconds.

### Hoist Operator / Crew Chief
Positioned at the open cabin door, the hoist operator manages the mechanical reel, observes rotor clearances, and acts as the pilot's external eyes.

* **Cable Control:** Extends and retracts the winch hook smoothly, ensuring the line does not snag on branches or building cornices.
* **Flight Guidance:** Delivers precise verbal vectoring to the pilot (e.g., *"Creep forward two meters, hold altitude"*).
* **Door Clearance:** Confirms that patient litters and medical personnel clear the skids before authorizing transition flight.

### Rescue Medic (Ground Lead)
The rescue specialist rides the hook down to the casualty, stabilizes the scene, and works through medical interventions before any extraction takes place.

* **Patient Care:** Performs primary surveys, applies interventions, and clears the casualty for flight.
* **Rigging:** Securely fastens harnesses or the rescue litter to the hoist hook.
* **Clearance Calls:** Gives the final go-ahead for cable retraction once the patient is physically prepped for transport.

## Step-by-Step Pilot Hover and Winch Execution

Holding a motionless hover while a human swings 80 feet below your aircraft is one of the steepest learning curves in the simulator. Executing this maneuver cleanly demands an established rhythm. 

```
[1. Approach Upwind] ──► [2. Stabilize Drift] ──► [3. Call "Stable"]
                                                        │
[6. Safe Vertical Climb] ◄── [5. Reel & Secure] ◄── [4. Winch Descent]
```

To complete this procedure reliably, follow this operational checklist:

1. **Wind-Corrected Approach:** Always approach the hover point facing directly into the ambient wind vector. Headwinds provide free lift and dramatically decrease horizontal wandering.
2. **Pre-Hover Deceleration:** Bleed off forward airspeed 150 meters before reaching the victim. Drop collective gradually with `Ctrl` to arrive slow and high.
3. **Station Keeping:** Bring groundspeed to zero directly over the target. Fix your vision on a static reference point, such as a distant tree line or rock outcropping, to detect uncommanded drift.
4. **Authorizing Cable Deployment:** Once the cyclic and collective are settled, call `"On station, stable."` The hoist operator now deploys the medic.
5. **Night Hoist Adjustments:** If flying night operations, activate night vision using `N`. Maintain extra altitude above power lines and tree canopies, as depth perception degrades under NVG rendering.
6. **Smooth Ingress & Outgress:** When the casualty reaches the cabin, avoid immediately tilting the nose down. Climb straight up vertically by 50 to 100 feet until you clear surrounding obstacles, then smoothly pitch forward into forward cruise flight.

| Flight Phase | Pilot Focus | Operator Action | Medic Action | Key Safety Metric |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Approach** | Headwind alignment, slow descent | Cabin door open, system armed | Harness check, tools prepped | Airspeed < 15 kts at 100m |
| **Hover Hold** | Zero translational drift, power check | Cable payout initiated | Rides hook down to terrain | Vertical variance < 3 feet |
| **On-Deck Care** | Monitor fuel bingo, listen for alerts | Observes cable angle and slack | Diagnoses file, applies triage | 60-second pilot fuel updates |
| **Recovery** | Micro collective tweaks, freeze cyclic | Reels winch at continuous rate | Guides litter clear of skids | Zero aircraft pitch changes |
| **Departure** | Pure vertical climb, then forward tilt | Cabin secured, hoist stowed | Secondary vitals check in cabin | Clear 100ft obstacle bubble |

## Medical Care: The Mandatory Triage Protocol

A common rookie mistake highlighted in community reports involves trying to reel a patient into the helicopter before treating their injuries. In this simulator, the winch is strictly an extraction tool—it does not bypass treatment requirements. Casualties will flatline or fail the boarding check if their vital conditions are ignored on the ground.

Before the hoist hook can lift a victim upward, the ground medic must open the victim’s medical file and resolve critical injuries under the rotor downwash.

| Medical Equipment | Clinical Indication | In-Game Trigger / Symptom | Operational Priority |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cervical Collar** | Suspected spinal trauma, fall from height | Blunt trauma callouts, cliffside falls | Apply prior to moving patient |
| **Tourniquet** | Severe arterial hemorrhage | Rapidly dropping blood volume bar | Immediate; within first 15 seconds |
| **Traction Splint** | Compound or closed limb fractures | Deformed limb icons, mobility flags | Stabilize prior to litter hookup |
| **Intubation Probe** | Obstructed airway, respiratory arrest | Zero airway reading, agonal respirations | Urgent; secures breathing gate |
| **Respirator / Bag** | Hypoxia, failing oxygenation saturation | Low SpO2 warning below critical threshold | Mandatory before cabin boarding |

Always execute ground care methodically. Trying to rush medical interventions while the pilot struggles with hovering usually results in incomplete treatments, failed objectives, or fuel exhaustion.

## Helicopter Selection and Flight Dynamics

Selecting the proper airframe greatly simplifies hover stability. Beginners often make the mistake of jumping straight into heavy, twin-engine turbine helicopters that exhibit heavy cyclic inertia. Starting with lighter, more forgiving airframes allows you to build fine collective control without fighting excessive pendulum swings.

Review the structural and flight characteristics of the primary rescue airframes below:

| Airframe Class | Handling Difficulty | Hover Stability | Fuel Burn Rate | Recommended Role |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Light Utility (e.g., EC135 class)** | Low | High (Snappy response) | Moderate (~8 lbs/min) | Mountain rescue, rooftop extractions |
| **Intermediate Twin (e.g., EC145 / SAMU)** | Moderate | Very High (Balanced mass) | High (~12 lbs/min) | Multi-casualty triage, long-line winching |
| **Heavy Lift / Maritime** | High | Low (High inertia lag) | Very High (~18+ lbs/min) | Offshore water rescues, mass casualty events |

For deeper technical specifications regarding collective authority and aerodynamic modeling across civilian aircraft, consult the official [Federal Aviation Administration Helicopter Flying Handbook](https://www.faa.gov/regulations_policies/handbooks_manuals/aviation/helicopter_flying_handbook).

## Standard Communication Protocols

Crisp, standardized radio phraseology prevents fatal misunderstandings. When rotors are spinning at 400 RPM and engines are running at maximum continuous power, ambiguity gets crew members killed. Keep callouts short, clear, and direct.

```
       [ PILOT ]                        [ CREW ]
  "On station, stable"   ────────►  (Begins Winch Descent)
                                           │
  (Holds Position)       ◄────────  "Cable going out"
                                           │
  (Monitors Bingo)       ◄────────  "Medic on deck, file open"
                                           │
  (Acknowledges Status)  ◄────────  "Patient packaged, coming up"
                                           │
  (Climbs Vertically)    ◄────────  "In cabin, cable clear"
```

* **Pilot:** *"On station, stable."* (Confirms the aircraft is locked in space; permissions are open to deploy.)
* **Hoist Operator:** *"Cable going out."* (Warns the pilot that a live weight is shifting beneath the center of gravity.)
* **Rescue Medic:** *"On deck, file open."* (Medic has touched ground and begun casualty diagnostic assessment.)
* **Rescue Medic:** *"Triage complete, ready for hook."* (Informs the operator that all required treatments are finished.)
* **Hoist Operator:** *"Load incoming, hold hover."* (Alerts the pilot to hold collective steady as the winch engine takes the load.)
* **Hoist Operator:** *"In cabin, cable secure, clear to climb."* (Confirms the casualty is inside the doors and the hoist hook is stowed.)
* **Pilot:** *"Climbing out."* (Pilot executes vertical departure before transitioning to cruise.)

## Emergency Abort Criteria and Safety Margins

Hovering consumes more fuel per minute than high-speed forward cruise because the rotors operate outside of effective translational lift (ETL). If an extraction takes too long, your fuel reserves will rapidly drop toward critical levels. 

Set clear abort parameters before every single sortie. If any of the following triggers occur, immediately break off the evolution:

1. **Fuel Bingo Threshold:** You hit the predetermined fuel reserve needed to return safely to the nearest refueling pad.
2. **Uncontrolled Cyclic Drift:** The pilot moves more than three meters off the hover target and cannot arrest lateral motion within three seconds.
3. **Fire / Thermal Penetration:** Shifting winds blow smoke plumes or convective heat columns across your hover path, threatening engine performance.
4. **Loss of Visual Contact:** The hoist operator loses sight of the cable line or the medic due to nightfall, brownout dust, or whiteout conditions.

If an abort is called using the brevity code *"Abort hoist, break break,"* the winch operator halts cable feed immediately. Depending on altitude, the medic either unhooks instantly on the ground or prepares for an emergency extraction to a safe staging zone.

## Practice Drills to Build Muscle Memory

Mastery of rescue flight mechanics comes through deliberate training rather than stumbling through active career emergencies. Use these progressive drills in empty practice sessions to hone your flight skills:

```
[Level 1: 30s Static Hover]
           │
           ▼
[Level 2: Box Drift Control]
           │
           ▼
[Level 3: Blind Winch Reel]
           │
           ▼
[Level 4: Night Mountain Extraction]
```

* **Level 1 (Static Pad Hover):** Position your helicopter five feet above a painted helipad marking. Hold that hover for 30 consecutive seconds without allowing the skids to touch down or drift outside the yellow boundary lines.
* **Level 2 (Box Drift):** Hover over a runway and deliberately shift your aircraft five meters forward, five meters right, five meters backward, and five meters left. Pause for five seconds at each corner while holding a steady altitude.
* **Level 3 (Unloaded Cable Run):** Hover at 100 feet over a target obstacle. Deploy and retract the hoist cable without drifting laterally more than two meters.
* **Level 4 (Full Night Mission):** Fly into high-altitude terrain under night-vision goggles (`N`), hold a hover over a rugged ridge, and complete a mock casualty packaging run under strict fuel bingo limits.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Why won't the victim board the helicopter after I hook them to the hoist?
In Rotor Ops Rescue Fire Police, victims cannot board until all necessary field medical care is completed. You must open their medical dossier and apply required interventions—such as cervical collars, tourniquets, splints, or airway intubation—before the game clears the patient for cabin loading.

### What is the best helicopter for beginners to learn hoist operations?
Light-to-medium twin-engine helicopters like the EC135 or EC145 classes offer the best balance of collective responsiveness and cyclic stability. Heavier helicopters feature high control latency, which often causes rookie pilots to overcorrect during stationary hovers.

### How do I stop my helicopter from drifting sideways while winching?
Always turn the nose of your aircraft directly into the wind before establishing your hover. Use tiny, deliberate taps of your cyclic controls rather than holding down inputs, and focus your eyes on an external horizon point to catch lateral drift early.

### Does this rotor ops rescue fire police tutorial apply to solo gameplay?
Yes. While running with a human crew is ideal, solo players can still execute hoist rescues by mastering pilot hover stability, setting safe hover heights, and rapidly swapping positions or issuing commands to handle winch mechanics and ground medical care.
